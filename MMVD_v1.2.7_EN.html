Simple Summary

Myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD) is the most common heart disease in dogs and progresses to congestive heart failure in its late stages. Veterinary practice depends on echocardiography for diagnosis and staging, but the equipment and expertise barriers are high, and early-stage calls are often borderline. Can blood biomarkers (such as NT-proBNP and cardiac troponin I) help determine whether a dog already has heart disease? This systematic review pooled 18 diagnostic data sets from 9 studies and systematically compiled the evidence on biomarkers and echocardiographic indices for predicting death. Overall, biomarkers showed moderate diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity ≈ 0.74, specificity ≈ 0.76); they performed better for identifying congestive failure than for identifying heart enlargement, and the choice of cutoff strongly influenced the results — cutoffs optimized within the same dogs inflate apparent accuracy. Biomarkers should complement, not replace, echocardiography: they adjust pre-test probability and inform prognosis but should not trigger or withhold treatment on their own. Standardized cutoffs and prospective multicenter cohorts are the priority for future research.

Abstract

Myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD) is the most common cardiac disease in dogs; its diagnosis and staging rely on echocardiography, and serum cardiac biomarkers have been proposed as adjunctive tools, but single-study results are inconsistent and have never been formally synthesized. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the diagnostic accuracy of serum cardiac biomarkers for identifying stage B2 cardiac remodeling and congestive heart failure (CHF) in dogs with MMVD, and to synthesize the prognostic value of biomarkers and echocardiographic indices for mortality. PubMed (n = 142), Scopus (n = 186) and CABI (n = 107) were searched on 27–28 August 2026; 226 de-duplicated records entered screening and 74 underwent full-text assessmentprotocol registration pending (D2). Risk of bias was assessed with QUADAS-2 and QUIPS; paired sensitivity and specificity were pooled with a bivariate random-effects model. Nine studies provided 18 index-test combinations: overall sensitivity 0.738 (95% CI 0.662–0.802), specificity 0.761 (0.698–0.814), diagnostic odds ratio 8.96 (6.08–13.23), summary AUC 0.814. Accuracy was target-condition dependent: sensitivity for stage B2 identification was only 0.655 (0.538–0.756); for CHF identification, sensitivity 0.756 (0.634–0.847) and specificity 0.741 (0.674–0.799). NT-proBNP (DOR 11.33) outperformed cTnI (DOR 5.37); no significant publication bias was detected (Deek's P = 0.405). The narrative prognostic synthesis (7 studies) showed LA/Ao ≥ 2.2 predicted cardiac death (HR 2.334, 1.219–4.468), with concordant NT-proBNP associations in independent cohorts. Current evidence supports biomarkers as adjuncts to, not replacements for, echocardiography; cutoff standardization and prospective B2-stage cohorts are priority research directions.

Keywords: dog; myxomatous mitral valve disease; NT-proBNP; cardiac troponin I; diagnostic accuracy; systematic review; meta-analysis

1. Introduction

Myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD) is the most common cardiac disease in dogs, accounting for approximately three quarters of all acquired cardiovascular disorders in the species, with prevalence rising steeply with age and reaching the majority of small-breed dogs beyond ten years of age MMVD epidemiology and prevalence in small-breed dogs. The clinical course is classically described by the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine (ACVIM) staging scheme, which stratifies affected dogs from asymptomatic stage B1 (no cardiac remodeling) through stage B2 (remodeling without congestive signs) to stage C (current or prior congestive heart failure, CHF) and stage D (refractory CHF) ACVIM consensus statement MMVD staging 2019 [1]. Within this framework, echocardiography is the cornerstone of diagnosis and staging: it defines the left atrial and ventricular remodeling that separates B1 from B2 and thereby identifies the patients in whom initiation of pimobendan delays the onset of CHF, as established in the EPIC trial EPIC trial: pimobendan delays CHF in stage B2 [2]. Echocardiography nevertheless has practical limitations. It requires expensive equipment, operator training and experience, and anesthesia-free examination protocols that are not universally available in first-opinion practice limited access to echocardiography in primary care. Moreover, the B1–B2 threshold is inherently marginal: dogs with borderline measurements around the accepted cutoffs are difficult to stage reproducibly, even among experienced cardiologists interobserver variability and borderline B2 classification. These limitations create a genuine demand for accessible, objective adjunctive tests.

Circulating cardiac biomarkers offer one such adjunct and have a coherent pathophysiological basis. Myocardial and valvular remodeling in MMVD produces volume overload and progressive left-sided chamber dilation, leading to increased ventricular wall stretch and enhanced secretion of B-type natriuretic peptides; the more stable amino-terminal fragment, NT-proBNP, has therefore been evaluated extensively as a marker of cardiac remodeling and congestion NT-proBNP physiology and wall stretch in dogs. Conversely, myocyte injury and strain may release cardiac troponin I (cTnI) into the circulation, providing a complementary signal of myocardial damage cTnI as marker of myocardial injury in canine heart disease [3]. Additional candidates, including galectin-3 as a marker of fibrosis, have been explored in this setting galectin-3 and cardiac fibrosis in veterinary medicine [4]. The central clinical question, however, remains unresolved: are these biomarkers intended to replace echocardiography as a diagnostic test, or merely to assist clinical judgment as a probability-modifying tool that complements echocardiographic staging? How this question is answered determines the required accuracy standard, the acceptable cost, and the settings in which biomarker testing is appropriate.

Although individual studies of NT-proBNP and cTnI for detecting ACVIM stage B2 or CHF have been published over the past two decades, their conclusions are notably inconsistent, reflecting differences in target conditions, comparator groups, assay platforms, and—critically—in the cutoff values used to define test positivity, many of which were derived post hoc from the same populations being evaluated inconsistent conclusions among individual canine biomarker accuracy studies [5–9]. To date, no systematic review or formal meta-analysis has synthesized this evidence base, quantified summary accuracy with appropriate bivariate modeling, or appraised the methodological quality of the underlying studies. This constitutes a clear evidence gap: practitioners are left to interpret discordant single-study results without a pooled estimate of diagnostic performance, and researchers lack a structured account of the sources of heterogeneity that might inform the design of future studies absence of prior systematic reviews of canine cardiac biomarkers.

New (Oct 29): the second layer of the research gap — how evidence itself is produced in this field (from first-hand clinical observation)

Beyond inconsistency, a deeper and less often articulated limitation concerns the way evidence is produced in this field: most candidate biomarkers remain at the discovery stage. The typical design reports between-stage differences in concentration (analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis tests, P < 0.05) and then equates "significant group differences" with "clinical diagnostic value" — without ROC-derived cutoffs, without sensitivity or specificity, and therefore without any possibility of reconstructing a diagnostic 2×2 table examples of biomarker studies limited to between-group comparisons. On the prognostic side, some abstracts advertise longitudinal follow-up that is in fact cross-sectional: blood sampling demonstrates that concentrations rise with stage, but no hard clinical endpoint and no multivariable Cox proportional-hazards model are provided — and without endpoint events or hazard ratios, no quantitative synthesis is possible. The bottleneck of this field is therefore not merely that studies disagree, but that a systematic translation gap separates discovery-phase findings from poolable diagnostic or prognostic evidence. This is precisely why the present review required reconstructable 2×2 data or adjusted effect estimates for inclusion, and why its conclusions carry explicit reporting-standard recommendations.

The aim of the present study was therefore to systematically review and meta-analyze the diagnostic accuracy of serum cardiac biomarkers (NT-proBNP, cTnI, and related markers) against the ACVIM staging scheme for identifying dogs with stage B2 remodeling or CHF, using bivariate random-effects modeling of sensitivity and specificity, and to narratively synthesize the prognostic value of these biomarkers and of echocardiographic indices for cardiac mortality. Importantly, echocardiography was deliberately excluded as an index test: because the ACVIM gold standard itself is defined by echocardiographic criteria, incorporating echo-based tests would introduce an incorporation bias that inflates apparent accuracy. This methodological decision—treating echocardiography as part of the reference standard and the prognostic evidence base rather than as a diagnostic index test—was specified before pooled estimates were computed, and target conditions were analyzed separately (stage B2 versus CHF) because they represent clinically and therapeutically distinct questions.

2.2 Search strategy

Three databases were searched: PubMed/MEDLINE (27 August 2026; n = 142), Scopus (28 August 2026; n = 186), and CABI VetMed Resource (28 August 2026; n = 107), yielding 435 records. The search combined four concept blocks — (i) canine; (ii) MMVD and its historical synonyms (degenerative mitral valve disease, endocardiosis, mitral regurgitation); (iii) cardiac biomarkers (natriuretic peptides, troponins, and related markers); (iv) echocardiographic parameters — with a fifth block of diagnostic-accuracy and prognosis filters. The full, verbatim search strings as executed in each database are provided in Appendix S1.P0 pending: verbatim executed search strings — export from Zotero/database search history Records were merged and de-duplicated in Zotero on 29 August 2026, leaving 226 unique records.

2.3 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they (i) enrolled dogs with naturally occurring MMVD staged by the ACVIM scheme (or an explicitly described equivalent clinical staging); (ii) evaluated a serum or other biological-matrix cardiac biomarker against that staging; and (iii) reported data allowing reconstruction of a 2×2 table (true/false positives/negatives) against a stated target condition, or adjusted prognostic effect estimates (hazard ratios) for mortality. Echocardiographic measurements were not eligible as index tests, because the reference standard itself is defined by echocardiographic criteria; evaluating an echo-derived test against an echo-defined standard would constitute incorporation bias. Echocardiographic and biomarker variables were instead eligible as prognostic factors. Reviews, editorials, case reports, interventional studies, and studies of non-cardiac biomarkers without a cardiac target condition were excluded.

2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts of the 226 unique records were screened, and 74 reports proceeded to full-text assessment. Full-text reports were classified as included (n = 12), excluded with documented reasons (n = 55), or sought for retrieval (n = 7)decision D3: single/double independent screening and disagreement adjudication. From each diagnostic study we extracted study characteristics, index test details (biomarker, matrix, assay, cutoff), and the 2×2 counts against each target condition, yielding 18 index-test combinations from 9 studies (Table 2). Cutoffs prespecified as primary were the high-specificity cutoff (≥1138 pmol/L) of Wesselowski et al. for stage B2 identification and the disease-spectrum comparator (stage C vs B1) of Klein et al. for CHF identification; healthy-control comparators and high-sensitivity cutoffs were retained for sensitivity analyses. 2.5 Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias of the nine diagnostic studies was assessed with QUADAS-2 (four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, flow and timing). Prognostic studies were assessed with QUIPS (six domains). QUADAS-2 was chosen over the JBI checklist (used in the professor's cat HCM draft) because it is designed specifically for diagnostic accuracy studies and recommended by the Cochrane DTA Handbook; the two drafts address different study types (SMD mean comparisons vs DTA), so each tool suits its design.if D1 adopts the separate prognostic arm, QUIPS here covers 7 reports

2.6 Statistical analysis

Paired sensitivity and specificity were pooled with the Reitsma bivariate random-effects model (R package mada 0.5.12); summary points, 95% confidence and prediction regions in SROC space, DOR, PLR, NLR, and the Rutter–Gatsonis AUC were derived from the model. Estimates were cross-validated against the Chu–Cole glmer formulation (lme4, nAGQ = 1), identical to the MetaDTA web application; agreement was ≤0.021 on the probability scale for all pooled sets. Prespecified subgroup analyses were (i) by target condition — Group A: identification of stage B2 remodeling; Group B: identification of CHF; Group C: MMVD diagnosis against healthy controls (descriptive only, single combination); and (ii) by biomarker (NT-proBNP; cTnI). Heterogeneity was explored through cutoff-strategy and comparator-type sensitivity analyses. Publication bias was examined with Deek's funnel-plot asymmetry test. For the prognostic synthesis, quantitative pooling was prespecified to require ≥3 comparable adjusted logHRs from independent studies for a given predictor–outcome pair; otherwise evidence was synthesized narrativelya standardized synthesis framework (e.g., SWiM) will be cited here. Analyses were performed in R 4.6.1; statistical significance was α = 0.05.

3. Results

3.1 Study selection

The search identified 435 records (PubMed 142, Scopus 186, CABI 107); after de-duplication, 226 unique records were screened at title/abstract level and 74 proceeded to full-text assessment (Figure 1). Of these, 12 studies were included, 55 were excluded (off-target biomarker, n = 34; no quantitative 2×2 endpoint, n = 18; other, n = 3) (Table S1), and 7 reports remained sought for retrieval, of which 2 were judged potentially extractable from open sources. Nine of the 12 included studies provided 2×2 data (18 index-test combinations); three provided prognostic models only.

3.3 Risk of bias

QUADAS-2 (Figure 2, Figure S1) identified the index test domain as the principal limitation: cutoffs were derived post hoc from the same population under evaluation in four studies (high risk) and were unclear in two. Patient selection was high risk in one case–control study with population imbalance. The reference-standard and flow-and-timing domains were low risk in all nine studies; three studies were rated low risk across all domains. QUIPS ratings of the prognostic studies were of moderate quality, with confounding control the most common limitation.

3.4 Overall diagnostic accuracy

Across all 18 combinations (9 studies), pooled sensitivity was 0.738 (95% CI 0.662–0.802) and pooled specificity 0.761 (0.698–0.814) (Figure 3). The summary DOR was 8.96 (6.08–13.23), with PLR 3.09 (2.46–3.87) and NLR 0.344 (0.268–0.442); the summary AUC was 0.814 (Figure 4). Estimates from the glmer cross-validation agreed with the bivariate model to within 0.021.

3.5 Accuracy by target condition

For stage B2 remodeling identification (Group A; 5 combinations, 4 studies), sensitivity was 0.655 (0.538–0.756) and specificity 0.783 (0.662–0.870); AUC 0.769 (Figures 5, 7A). For CHF identification (Group B; 9 combinations, 5 studies), sensitivity was 0.756 (0.634–0.847) and specificity 0.741 (0.674–0.799); AUC 0.795 (Figures 6, 7B). The single combination addressing overall MMVD diagnosis against healthy controls (Galectin-3) yielded 0.70/0.90 and was not pooled.

3.6 Accuracy by biomarker

NT-proBNP (10 combinations, 7 studies): sensitivity 0.780 (0.690–0.850), specificity 0.762 (0.671–0.834), DOR 11.33 (7.52–17.05), AUC 0.837. cTnI (5 combinations, 3 studies): sensitivity 0.638 (0.412–0.816), specificity 0.753 (0.643–0.838), DOR 5.37 (1.80–16.00), AUC 0.768 (Figures 8, 9). Galectin-3, NT-proANP and uALPc each contributed a single combination and are reported descriptively without modeling.

3.9 Prognostic synthesis

Seven studies reported extractable prognostic effect estimates: three from the included set (Hezzell 2012; Iwasa 2023; Channgam 2024) and four identified among reports excluded under the diagnostic criteria (Guglielmini 2021; Park 2026; Iwasa 2025; Masters 2026)decision marker D1, see end of manuscript. No predictor–outcome pair met the prespecified pooling threshold (≥3 comparable adjusted logHRs from independent studies); quantitative pooling was therefore not attempted. Directionally consistent findings were:

• Left atrial size. LA/Ao ≥ 2.2 predicted cardiac death with an adjusted HR of 2.334 (95% CI 1.219–4.468) (Park 2026); LA/Ao predicted 6-month all-cause death (HR 1.892, 1.232–2.905) (Guglielmini 2021); LA/Ao ×10 predicted all-cause death (HR 1.22, 1.14–1.31, unadjusted) (Channgam 2024).

• Ventricular dimensions. LVEDDN (×10) predicted all-cause (HR 1.23, 1.13–1.34) and cardiac death (HR 1.41, 1.24–1.60) (Hezzell 2012); LVIDDN showed large unadjusted effects (HR 4.669, 1.752–12.44) (Iwasa 2023) but lost significance in adjusted models (Park 2026).

• NT-proBNP. Two independent cohorts reported concordant adjusted associations with cardiac death (HR 1.07 per 100 pmol/L, 1.03–1.11, Hezzell 2012; HR 1.04 per 100 pmol/L, 1.01–1.06, Channgam 2024).

• cTnI. hs-cTnI > 0.025 ng/mL predicted all-cause death (HR 1.82, 1.08–3.06) (Hezzell 2012).

• No study modeled incident CHF as a survival endpoint, and Iwasa 2025 reported restricted mean survival time differences (NT-proBNP > 2230 pmol/L: ≈187 days lost within 1 year) because the proportional-hazards assumption failed.

This paragraph: ① main findings overview — pooled accuracy, target-condition and biomarker differences, prognostic direction, stated once

In this systematic review and bivariate meta-analysis of serum cardiac biomarkers for diagnosing canine MMVD, we identified 12 eligible studies from 435 records (226 after de-duplication), of which nine contributed 18 index-test combinations to the diagnostic meta-analysis and three reported prognostic data in the included set. Pooled across all biomarkers and target conditions, the summary sensitivity was 0.738 (95% CI 0.662–0.802) and the summary specificity was 0.761 (0.698–0.814), with a diagnostic odds ratio of 8.96 (6.08–13.23), a positive likelihood ratio of 3.09, a negative likelihood ratio of 0.344, and a summary AUC of 0.814. Accuracy differed meaningfully by target condition: for identifying stage B2 cardiac remodeling, sensitivity was only 0.655 (0.538–0.756) against a specificity of 0.783 (0.662–0.870) and AUC 0.769, whereas for identifying CHF, sensitivity rose to 0.756 (0.634–0.847) but specificity fell to 0.741 (0.674–0.799) and AUC was 0.795. By biomarker, NT-proBNP showed a pooled DOR of 11.33 (7.52–17.05) and cTnI a pooled DOR of 5.37 (1.80–16.00). Heterogeneity was dominated by cutoff strategy and comparator composition, and the narrative prognostic synthesis indicated that echocardiographic indices of left atrial enlargement predict cardiac death, with limited but concordant evidence that biomarker elevation carries prognostic information.

This paragraph: ② comparison with previous single studies — translating qualitative conclusions into quantitative context; the field's inconsistency is partly self-inflicted

Our pooled estimates are broadly consistent with—while contextualizing—the qualitative conclusions of the individual studies that have populated this literature. Wilshaw 2021 and Klein 2022 generally concluded that NT-proBNP concentration increases with advancing ACVIM stage and can discriminate remodeled from non-remodeled dogs, but both emphasized imperfect discrimination at the individual level and dependence on the chosen threshold Wilshaw 2021 NT-proBNP across ACVIM stages [5]. Wesselowski 2023 similarly reported that natriuretic peptides perform acceptably for distinguishing congested from non-congested patients but stressed assay and cutoff variability Wesselowski 2023 natriuretic peptide CHF discrimination [7]. Ogawa 2021 reported encouraging discrimination for B2 detection in a cohort of small-breed dogs while noting the small sample and the need for external validation Ogawa 2021 biomarker detection of stage B2 [8]. Schober 2010, one of the earliest rigorously designed studies, concluded that NT-proBNP was informative but emphasized that healthy dogs and dogs with early disease overlap substantially Schober 2010 early NT-proBNP validation study [9]. Our synthesis converts these qualitative statements into a quantitative position: the tests are genuinely informative (DOR 8.96) but neither the near-perfect discriminator hoped for by enthusiasts nor the useless test dismissed by skeptics; the observed summary operating point sits in the moderate range, and the between-study dispersion visible in the SROC plane is explained largely by design choices rather than by biology alone. In line with the caveats voiced in the primary studies, our subgroup analyses attribute much of the heterogeneity to cutoff strategy and comparator composition, confirming that the field's inconsistency is partly self-inflicted.

This paragraph: ③ boundary of cardiorenal-axis adjacent markers — why SDMA and allies did not enter, and how that strengthens the natriuretic/troponin focus

It is worth delimiting the scope of this synthesis with respect to markers adjacent to the cardiorenal axis. Several studies of symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) and related renal markers in MMVD were identified at the full-text stage and did not enter the meta-analysis: serum cystatin C and SDMA in dogs with congestive failure (Choi 2017), SDMA reliability across ACVIM stages (Savarese 2018), plasma dimethylarginines across disease severity (Valente 2020; Valente 2021), and the renal resistive index combined with SDMA and cystatin C (Szczepankiewicz 2021). None of these reported receiver-operating-characteristic analyses with derived cutoffs against ACVIM target conditions, so no 2×2 table could be reconstructed; where group comparisons were performed, SDMA did not discriminate MMVD stages (Savarese 2018; Valente 2020), and no adjusted survival models were available. SDMA therefore remains a marker of renal function rather than of cardiac remodeling in this disease, and its negative or non-quantitative record in MMVD reinforces—rather than weakens—the focus of the present synthesis on natriuretic peptides and cardiac troponins SDMA as GFR marker in dogs [12]; negative SDMA findings in canine cardiology [13–16].

New (Oct 29): why most studies in this field cannot enter quantitative synthesis — a systematic failure-mapping of the 55 excluded reports ("evidence ecology")

Classifying the 55 excluded reports one by one allowed this review to move beyond enumerating exclusion reasons toward characterizing recurrent failure modes in the field's evidence production. Three patterns recurred. First, pseudo-longitudinal designs: several abstracts advertised prognostic or longitudinal follow-up, yet the underlying studies were cross-sectional comparisons — blood sampling demonstrating that concentrations "increase with stage" — without hard clinical endpoints (cardiac death, incident CHF) or multivariable Cox models, and hence without endpoint events or hazard ratios, leaving nothing for quantitative synthesis. Second, misalignment between marker and clinical question: many studies measured organ-specific or systemic markers (red-cell distribution width, lipase, endothelin-1, soluble ST2, nitrotyrosine [21]) RDW and pulmonary hypertension [17,18]; ET-1 in canine heart disease [19]; sST2 in MMVD [20] that do not intersect with an ACVIM-anchored diagnostic question and were excluded as off-target. Third, fragmented reference frameworks: some reports substituted a single echocardiographic parameter (e.g., mitral regurgitant jet area) or ad hoc grading schemes for the full ACVIM system, precluding ROC computation against a common target condition or pooling with the present dataset. These excluded directions are not without value — soluble ST2 and galectin-3 (fibrosis axis) and endothelin-1 (pulmonary vascular–right heart axis) each correspond to coherent pathophysiological pathways — they have simply not yet been placed within the study template that anchors an ACVIM reference standard, hard endpoints, and prespecified cutoffs. The mapping suggests that what the field lacks is not new biomarkers but a unified research framework capable of converting candidate markers into synthesizable evidence.

This paragraph: ④ clinical meaning of target-condition stratification — B2 not usable as stand-alone screening, CHF misclassification risk, threshold-optimization bias, external validation as prerequisite (the paper's most consequential paragraph)

The stratification by target condition carries the most consequential clinical message of this review. For identifying stage B2 remodeling—the decision point at which pimobendan is initiated—the pooled sensitivity of 0.655 is clearly insufficient for biomarkers to function as an independent screening test: at this sensitivity, a meaningful fraction of dogs eligible for disease-modifying therapy would be missed if biomarkers were used alone. A negative result at this operating point cannot be used to defer echocardiography. The CHF target condition performed somewhat better (sensitivity 0.756), but at the price of reduced specificity (0.741), meaning that in the emergency or triage setting a positive biomarker result would still misclassify a non-trivial proportion of dogs as congested, with obvious risks of inappropriate anti-congestive treatment. These trade-offs must be understood in light of the threshold optimization bias captured by the index test domain of QUADAS-2, which was the most compromised domain (four studies judged at high risk and two at unclear risk): most studies selected cutoffs post hoc from the same population being evaluated, a practice that inflates apparent accuracy and transfers poorly to new populations. Our sensitivity analyses make this concrete. Within the B2 subgroup, applying higher-sensitivity cutoffs shifted the operating point to a sensitivity of 0.728 with a specificity of 0.708, and including healthy controls in the comparator pool inflated the pooled DOR from 8.86 to 10.69—both indicating that published accuracy estimates are sensitive to analysis choices and that routinely quoted values may be optimistically biased. External validation with prespecified, transportable cutoffs is therefore a prerequisite before any accuracy figure from this literature is adopted into clinical decision rules. Oct 29 newA more fundamental cost is inherent to the DTA framework itself: meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy requires dichotomizing continuous concentration variables, and every dichotomization irreversibly discards dose–response information — the monotonic discriminative structure a marker may carry across its full concentration range is reduced to a single operating point. The cutoff-strategy sensitivity we observed compounds this information compression: published accuracy estimates depend not only on where a cutoff came from, but on whether a single cutoff is posited at all.

This paragraph: ⑤ repositioning echocardiography — why echo was excluded as an index test (circular logic) and its prognostic value (the paper's methodological highlight)

A second methodological message concerns the position of echocardiography itself. We deliberately excluded echocardiographic index tests from the meta-analysis because the ACVIM reference standard is defined by echocardiographic findings; evaluating an echo-based test against an echo-defined gold standard produces incorporation bias, inflating sensitivity and specificity toward values that reflect circular logic rather than independent diagnostic information. To our knowledge, prior narrative reviews in this area have not made this distinction explicit, and the inclusion of echo-derived index tests would have rendered the pooled estimates uninterpretable. This is not a dismissal of echocardiography—quite the opposite. Our prognostic synthesis demonstrates that echocardiographic measurements retain strong, well-characterized prognostic value: left atrial size expressed as LA/Ao ≥ 2.2 predicted cardiac death with an HR of 2.334 (95% CI 1.219–4.468) Park 2026 [22], and normalized left ventricular internal diameter carried prognostic information Hezzell 2012 [23]. Because no pair of prognostic studies was sufficiently homogeneous to meet our criteria for quantitative pooling, we synthesized this evidence narratively. Within it, two independent studies reported concordant associations between elevated NT-proBNP and cardiac death, suggesting that biomarkers may add prognostic—rather than purely diagnostic—value to echocardiographic staging. The resulting framework is coherent: echocardiography is simultaneously the structural anchor of the reference standard and the strongest prognostic instrument, while biomarkers occupy the complementary roles of probability modification and prognostic enrichment. Oct 29 newThis framing also answers the original clinical question of this review: the goal of biomarkers was never to replace echocardiography but to demonstrate incremental value over echocardiography alone — that is, whether a marker carries independent information in multivariable models adjusting for continuous echocardiographic variables (LA/Ao, LVIDDN). The prognostic synthesis provides a preliminary affirmative direction (NT-proBNP remained concordantly associated with cardiac death across two independent cohorts), yet studies meeting the "continuous echocardiographic variables plus biomarkers" design standard remain few in number — the very gap future work should target.

New (Oct 29, second addition): re-orienting clinical practice — staging as the skeleton for learning and structured management, heart-failure recognition as the bedside decision core; and the not-yet-established boundary of breed-standardized reference values

Returning to the realities of companion-animal practice, these results point to a re-orientation of clinical focus worth stating explicitly Oct 29 new: in the day-to-day diagnosis of canine cardiac disease, the question clinicians actually need to answer is usually not "is this dog stage B2 or stage C," but "is it in, or about to enter, congestive heart failure now". The value of the ACVIM staging system is beyond dispute — it is structurally clear, teachable, and reproducible, and it forms the basic skeleton for learning and for structured management in veterinary cardiology, with follow-up intervals, drug initiation, and dose-escalation decisions all built on stage; but staging is fundamentally a static morphological position, whereas congestion is a dynamic functional state, and the two do not share a time scale. The target-condition stratification in this review provides quantitative support at precisely this point: the combined information from biomarkers and echocardiography identifies CHF (sensitivity 0.756, AUC 0.795) better overall than it identifies stage B2 remodeling (sensitivity 0.655, AUC 0.769). In other words, the comparative advantage of combining cardiac biomarkers with echocardiography is precisely to shift clinical attention from a stage label to immediate recognition of the heart-failure state. We therefore propose the following division of labor: serious, structured management built on staging — which answers "where do we start, and at what rhythm do we manage"; and continuous, biomarker-plus-echocardiographic assessment to answer the bedside question — "is the dog in heart failure now, should treatment be adjusted, should re-examination be moved up". This division also answers a third question Oct 29 new: do the data support looking at only one of the two modalities, or prioritizing one? The available evidence answers this clearly, and with a direction. Biomarkers alone are not supported — a sensitivity of 0.655 for the B2 target condition implies too high a miss rate, and under no target condition is there a basis for triggering or withholding therapy on biomarker results alone (see the clinical implications of target-condition stratification above); echocardiography first is supported — echo is already the structural anchor of the reference standard and the strongest prognostic instrument, and current practice of performing echo first is uncontroversial; the genuinely open question is whether biomarkers add anything after echocardiography — our prognostic synthesis gives a preliminarily affirmative direction (NT-proBNP carried adjusted information beyond echo in two independent cohorts), but the evidence is not yet saturated. The sequence supported by the data is therefore "echocardiography first, biomarkers as increment, staging as the skeleton" — neither an either-or choice nor biomarker-first. This is also self-consistent with the framework of the "repositioning echocardiography" paragraph above: in this setting, echo and biomarkers respectively carry the roles of structural/prognostic anchor and probability modification/dynamic monitoring, rather than competing with the staging system.

At the same time, the boundary of applicability of this interpretation must be drawn honestly Oct 29 new: breed. The study populations included in this review mix purebred dogs (predominantly small breeds) with mixed-breed dogs, and whether disease progression is consistent within a single pure breed, and between mixed-breed dogs of similar size, currently cannot be confirmed. Consequently, whether one can analyze measurements against standardized breed-specific values (such as size-corrected LVIDDN, VHS, and related indices) and derive directly usable reference cutoffs likewise cannot be concluded from the available evidence. This means that the pooled operating points reported here should be extrapolated across breeds with restraint; future studies should report cutoffs and accuracy stratified by breed/size, and explicitly test breed effects within joint "continuous echocardiographic variable + biomarker" models — connecting directly to the third limitation (cutoff and breed-effect heterogeneity could not be formally examined) and to the research agenda in the Conclusions.

This paragraph: ⑥ limitations — six points (small evidence base, pending reports, cutoff heterogeneity, unsaturated prognostic evidence, no prospective registration, English-only search); publication-bias residual risk

This study has several limitations. First, the evidence base is small: only nine studies with usable 2×2 data (18 index-test combinations) could be pooled, and the number of studies within biomarker-by-target-condition subgroups limits the precision of subgroup estimates and our ability to model covariates in meta-regression. Second, retrieval of seven reports classified as pending was incomplete; two were judged potentially extractable from open sources and remain to be incorporated, and data from them could alter the pooled estimates or risk-of-bias judgments. Third, cutoff heterogeneity was pervasive and only partially addressable through sensitivity analyses; the cutoff strategies identified (including post hoc optimization) were too heterogeneous to support a formal investigation of assay-platform or breed effects beyond the subgroup analyses performed. Fourth, the prognostic evidence is not saturated: no predictor–outcome pair met the prespecified pooling threshold, hazard-ratio reporting was incomplete and inconsistently adjusted, and one study reported restricted mean survival times because the proportional-hazards assumption failed, so the prognostic conclusions must be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Fifth, the review protocol was not prospectively registered, which prevents verification that analysis decisions—including the target-condition stratification and the exclusion of echo-based index tests—were made entirely free of outcome knowledge. Sixth, the databases were searched through English-language interfaces and all included reports are published in English; relevant non-English studies may have been missed. Funnel-plot asymmetry testing was reassuring for the overall analysis (Deek's P = 0.405) but the NT-proBNP subset approached significance (P = 0.055), and small-study effects cannot be excluded in the subsets.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, serum cardiac biomarkers show moderate and clinically meaningful—but not stand-alone—diagnostic accuracy for identifying stage B2 cardiac remodeling and CHF in dogs with MMVD. We recommend that these tests be positioned as adjuncts for probability modification, used in conjunction with echocardiography rather than as its replacement: biomarker results should raise or lower pre-test suspicion but should not trigger or withhold pimobendan therapy, defer echocardiography, or establish a diagnosis of congestion on their own. Clinicians should interpret published accuracy estimates with awareness that cutoff optimization within the index populations and inflation from healthy controls have likely flattered the literature. The research agenda that follows from this review is specific: (i) adoption of standardized, externally derived and externally validated cutoffs, ideally species- and assay-specific, reported with full decision-analytic context; (ii) prospective cohort studies of asymptomatic dogs that enroll dogs at the B1–B2 boundary with blinded, protocolized echocardiographic staging, powered to estimate sensitivity at clinically relevant thresholds; and (iii) adequately adjusted prognostic studies with consistent hazard-ratio reporting, so that the diagnostic and prognostic value of cardiac biomarkers in canine MMVD can finally be quantified on a footing free of threshold optimization and incorporation bias. Oct 29 newBuilding on the systematic mapping of the excluded evidence, we further propose a set of minimum reporting standards: (a) hard endpoints (cardiac death, incident CHF) with adjusted hazard ratios, rather than P values for between-stage concentration differences alone; (b) ROC-derived AUC together with prespecified cutoffs and their sensitivity and specificity, so that results can enter quantitative synthesis; and (c) joint models of continuous echocardiographic variables and biomarkers as the design backbone, directly estimating incremental value over echocardiography alone. Three thematic routes correspond to the pathophysiological pathways of the excluded markers: the myocardial interstitial fibrosis axis (galectin-3, soluble ST2, GDF-15 — requiring standardized effect sizes and multivariable adjustment), the pulmonary vascular–right heart loading axis (endothelin-1 and related vasoactive markers — requiring multivariable Cox models and survival analysis to establish independent prognostic value), and the myocardial injury axis (cTnI — whose independent predictive role remains unstable in adjusted models and requires cutoff standardization, with linkage to cardiorenal markers such as cystatin C and urinary tubular-injury markers). Under the present inclusion criteria, none of these routes yet has poolable quantitative evidence; they therefore constitute not conclusions of this review but the research agenda it lays down for the next synthesis.

  1. Biomarkers have moderate diagnostic value but cannot stand alone — 18 combinations pooled: sensitivity 0.738, specificity 0.761, DOR 8.96, AUC 0.814. Positioning = probability-modifying adjuncts, combined with rather than replacing echocardiography.
  2. Accuracy must be read by target condition — sensitivity for B2 remodeling identification is only 0.655 (cannot justify deferring echo); CHF identification performs somewhat better (0.756/0.741) with real misclassification risk.
  3. NT-proBNP outperforms cTnI — DOR 11.33 vs 5.37; Galectin-3, NT-proANP and uALPc contributed single combinations and are reported descriptively.
  4. Published values are likely systematically inflated — post hoc cutoffs (QUADAS-2 index-test domain 4 high + 2 unclear), healthy-control inflation (DOR 8.86→10.69), and information loss from dichotomization; external validation with prespecified transportable cutoffs is the prerequisite for clinical adoption.
  5. Echo's position is a methodological decision, not a data gap — the ACVIM gold standard is echo-defined, making echo an index test circular; echo is the reference anchor and the strongest prognostic tool (LA/Ao ≥ 2.2 → cardiac death HR 2.334).
